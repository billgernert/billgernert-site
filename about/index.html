I am Bill Gernert, an infrastructure engineer in the Denver area with fifteen years of system administration behind me. Fifteen years of running other people’s infrastructure

I have spent fifteen years as a system administrator and infrastructure engineer, mostly in higher education and county government. The pattern across all of it is the same. I find the work people do by hand every week, turn it into something that runs on its own, and make it report when it fails.

Senior systems administrator, Jefferson County Business Innovation and Technology, 2021 to 2026

Grew a Jenkins automation platform from 20 scripts to 135 Python and PowerShell jobs, so routine requests ran self-service around the clock instead of arriving as tickets. After-hours support tickets fell by roughly 40 percent. I replaced a third party VDI monitoring product, which cost 30,000 dollars a year, with a PowerShell service that watched 350 virtual desktops, patched their images, and fixed the common failures without an operator in the loop. I removed 200 unused service accounts, moved the rest to group managed service accounts, and automated their credential rotation. Day to day I ran Active Directory, Entra ID, Exchange Online, Intune and SCCM for 2,000 users, and wired nine separate platforms together through their APIs so identity and certificate work stopped being manual.

Systems administrator, University of Colorado Denver, 2018 to 2021

Built a billing and reporting system that found compute nobody was being charged for across a 1,000 VM VMware estate, which recovered 10,000 dollars a month. Ran patching and configuration for 400 Windows servers and 15,000 workstations through SCCM and brought patch compliance lag down from weeks to 72 hours. Operated VMware Horizon VDI for 5,000 concurrent users, ran enterprise DNS in Infoblox across internal, external and affiliate zones, and built capacity forecasts that caught three resource shortfalls before they landed.

Manager of computer labs and classrooms, University of Colorado Denver, 2015 to 2018

Ran IT for 3 labs and 14 classrooms serving 2,000 students, and supervised 15 student employees. Writing down the procedures they followed cut helpdesk escalations by about 30 percent, which is the first time I learned that a documented process is itself a piece of infrastructure.

Colorado Army National Guard, 2014 to 2019

Executive officer, first for a 90 soldier aviation company and then for a space company of six teams and 44 soldiers. The aviation job included moving 100 pieces of equipment, helicopters included, across five states and onward to Kuwait and Iraq. Planning something that cannot be retried is a different discipline from planning something that can, and it is where the habit of writing the rollback before the change comes from.

What AutomationLab is

AutomationLab is a self-hosted platform I built and operate on a five-node Proxmox HA cluster, where every machine, secret, certificate and user account is defined in git and applied by a pipeline rather than by hand. It exists because a resume line that says “automated provisioning” is not checkable, and a pipeline that builds a server in front of you is. Everything else on this site is a piece of that lab, written up with the measured numbers and with the parts that broke left in.

How I work

Every change in the lab is a branch and a pull request that I review and merge myself, with yamllint, ansible-lint, shellcheck and gitleaks running as required checks before anything can land. Every failure becomes a backlog item carrying its evidence and a definition of done, so the same thing does not get fixed twice and forgotten twice.

Gates fail closed. Where a control cannot tell whether an action is safe, it stops instead of guessing. The decommission pipeline refuses to run when its plan would destroy nothing, because an empty plan succeeding looks exactly like a successful teardown.

And where something has been written but never actually run, the page describing it says so in those words. That rule costs the most and is the reason the rest is worth reading. A procedure nobody has walked is a plan, and calling it anything else is how you find out during the outage.
